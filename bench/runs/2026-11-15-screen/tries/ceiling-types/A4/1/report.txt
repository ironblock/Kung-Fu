I wrote `notes/over-the-top.json` with `eyeX ≈ -4.794`, `eyeY ≈ 8.776`, `upright: false` and `sameAsOtherSide: false`. The position is solid, but I'm not certain about the two `false` values: the declarations never say how the camera's up direction is chosen, and I couldn't check the library.

**Where it comes from:** The declarations only say the camera follows the Babylon.js convention (left-handed, alpha around Y, beta measured from the top). The usual Babylon.js formula is x = r·cos α·sin β, y = r·cos β. With alpha 0, beta -0.5 and radius 10, the camera ends up at (-4.794, 8.776, 0), on the far side of the model. With limits off, nothing clamps beta. The values may differ in the last few digits if the library stores its matrices in 32-bit floats.

**Why I said upside down:** Full Babylon.js flips the camera's up direction when sin(beta) is negative (its `allowUpsideDown` option, on by default). That is what you'd get from rolling continuously over the top, so the picture comes out upside down. The fresh `(Math.PI, 0.5)` camera sits in the same spot and looks the same way, but stays upright. The two views are a 180° roll apart, so they aren't the same.

**What I'm unsure of:** Babylon Lite's camera has no `upVector` or `allowUpsideDown` property in these declarations. If Lite always uses world +Y as up instead, both answers flip to `true`: the view would snap to match the other side, and you'd see a sudden half-turn at the pole. To check where the library is installed, put a point above the target through `getViewMatrix` and see whether its screen y goes up or down.
